# The buffer

Money left sitting in the joint account for the months that cost more than usual.
It is funded only from income nobody was counting on, so building it never makes a
month tighter than it would have been.

## The goal

A number of months of what the household costs to run: recurring costs, loan
interest and amortization, averaged over the coming twelve months. Averaged because
quarterly insurance and annual fees are exactly what a buffer absorbs, and a single
month's figure would miss them. One-off costs are excluded, as they are already
spread over their own months and pay themselves off.

Absent means one month. Zero means the household turned it off. They are different
things: a household that has never thought about it is better off with a small
buffer, while none is a choice someone made.

Any member can set it. It is a shared account and a shared responsibility, unlike
the split rule, which decides what each person owes.

## What a good month contributes

Two factors multiply, rather than taking turns:

```
share = 25% × goodness × room
  goodness   0 at normal income, 1 at half again as much
  room       1 on an empty account, 0 exactly at the goal
```

Multiplying is what makes the awkward case fall out on its own: a small excess
against a nearly full buffer rounds away to nothing without needing to be named.

Three things cap the result, and it is floored to the hundred so no cap can be
exceeded by the rounding meant to tidy it:

| Cap | Why |
|---|---|
| the share of the excess | it can only ever be money nobody expected |
| the gap to the goal | it stops on arrival rather than overshooting |
| the month's surplus | earning above normal and having anything spare are not the same thing |

The excess is measured on the household, not per member, or one person's bonus
would be taken partly out of the other's share.

## What it does not do

The trigger is income against normal income. Surplus is a ceiling, not the test:
making it the test would turn a slice of the unexpected into a standing charge on
every comfortable month.

The forecast assumes nothing. Future months are estimated at normal income and so
are never good ones, which keeps the line ahead of today free of guesses. A month
that has already gone well lifts every point after it.

There is no drawdown rule. The buffer is money in the account, so a lumpy month
spends it without being told to.

## Skipping a month

A month can decline its own contribution. The answer is kept on the household
rather than the device, because it changes what both people transfer. The note says
what the skip cost, so undoing it has a visible consequence, and it only appears
while there is something to restore.

## Absorbing a one-off

A one-off can be paid from what the account already holds and never charged back.
The money leaves in the starting month, nobody owes a share, and the balance stays
lower until good months rebuild it.

Offered only up to four fifths of what the account is expected to hold that month.
Absorbing more than it holds is not absorbing a cost, it is hiding one, and
absorbing all of it leaves the household with no buffer, which is what the buffer
was for.

The month's one-off figure still carries the whole amount, with a note naming the
part nobody repays, since that part is absent from every transfer underneath it.

## What it rests on

The goal is worked out from the costs recorded here and the gap from the balance
projected forward from the last reading anyone entered. Both are only right if
everything leaving that account is in the app. This is the one feature where a cost
nobody recorded turns into a wrong instruction about real money.
